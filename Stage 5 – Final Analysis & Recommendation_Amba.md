# **Stage 5 – Final Analysis & Recommendation**

The firm is exposed to foreign exchange risk because it expects to receive €4.5 million in one year from a European customer. This receivable is denominated in euros, and its value in U.S. dollars depends on the EUR/USD exchange rate at maturity. If the euro depreciates, the dollar value of the receivable will fall, which could reduce expected cash inflows and negatively affect liquidity forecasts and profitability. At today’s spot rate of 1.1642, the receivable is worth approximately $5.24 million, but the forward market rate of 1.0875 implies only $4.89 million, highlighting the risk of significant downside if the euro weakens.

The Stage 4 Excel model shows that the forward hedge would lock in $4,893,750, providing certainty but at a value below the current spot. The money market hedge produces $5,339,421, which is higher than the forward hedge and consistent with interest rate parity, though it requires upfront liquidity. The put option hedge establishes a floor of about $5,238,900, protecting against depreciation while allowing upside if the euro appreciates, but it requires paying a premium of $67,500. The collar strategy, which combines a put and a call, reduces the premium cost but caps upside potential, resulting in net proceeds of around $5.15 million. These outcomes demonstrate that the forward hedge offers certainty at a lower locked-in value, the money market hedge provides certainty with a higher locked-in value but requires liquidity, and the option hedge offers flexibility with downside protection at the cost of premiums.

When the euro depreciates, both the forward and money market hedges guarantee fixed dollar inflows, while the put option ensures a minimum floor value at the strike price, net of the premium. When the euro appreciates, the forward and money market hedges miss the upside, while the put option allows participation in appreciation, again net of the premium. The collar strategy caps upside gains in exchange for reduced premium costs. The key difference among these strategies is the trade-off between certainty and flexibility: the forward and money market hedges provide stability, while the option hedge preserves upside potential but at a cost.

Based on the analysis, the recommended strategy is the money market hedge. It provides the highest guaranteed dollar proceeds of approximately $5.34 million, passes the interest rate parity and equivalence checks in the Excel model, and aligns with the treasury objective of maximizing locked-in value while ensuring cash flow stability. Although it requires upfront borrowing, the firm’s balance sheet can accommodate this liquidity requirement, making it the most effective choice.

This recommendation is justified at the executive level because it ensures stable cash flows, supports accurate budgeting, and enhances shareholder confidence. It requires liquidity but avoids the premium costs associated with options. It also simplifies hedge accounting documentation compared to option strategies, which can be more complex to account for. The money market hedge therefore, balances certainty, cost efficiency, and operational feasibility.
